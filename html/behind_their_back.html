01 What this episode teaches

The podcast uses workplace gossip and relationship scenarios to teach informal expressions for gossip, hypocrisy, overstepping social boundaries, and manipulating relationships.

Core topic

뒷담화 / 뒷담 (dwitdamhwa / dwitdam) means talking negatively about someone who is not present. The shortened form 뒷담 is common in casual speech.

Register

Many expressions are casual, judgemental, or rude. They are useful for understanding dramas and real conversations, but are not neutral vocabulary for formal situations.

Interpretation warning

The transcript reports the host’s interpretation of a 2025 study about gossip and relationship satisfaction. The lesson explains what the speaker says; it does not independently verify the study’s findings.

Romanization note: Romanization is included as a reading aid, not as a perfect pronunciation guide. Korean sound changes can make the spoken form differ from a letter-by-letter rendering. Hangul remains the best guide once you can read it.

02 Guided breakdown by scene

Rather than translating every repeated filler word, this section breaks down the transcript’s key sentences and shows how the grammar works in natural speech.

Opening: defining 뒷담화

다른 사람이 없는 자리에서, 뒤에서 그 사람에 대해서 수군수군 안 좋게 나쁘게 말하는 걸 뒷담화한다고 하는데요.

Dareun sarami eomneun jarieseo, dwieseo geu sarame daehaeseo sugunsugun an jokke nappeuge malhaneun geol dwitdamhwahandago haneundeyo.

“Talking about someone negatively behind their back when they aren’t there is what we call 뒷담화.”

ChunkMeaning / function
다른 사람이 없는 자리에서in a situation/place where the other person isn’t present. -에서 marks the setting in which an action occurs.
그 사람에 대해서about that person. 에 대해서 means “about / concerning.”
수군수군Whispering or murmuring among people, often secretively. It is a mimetic/adverbial expression.
말하는 걸말하는 것 (“the act of saying/talking”) contracted to 말하는 걸 in speech; the object marker 을 becomes 걸 after contraction.
뒷담화한다고 하는데요뒷담화하다 + quoted-content marker -다고 하다 (“call/say that it is gossip”) + -는데요, which introduces or frames an explanation.
Listening tip: Spoken Korean frequently compresses 것을 → 걸, 것은 → 건, and 것이 → 게. Recognizing these contractions makes long sentences easier to parse.

“These are red flags”: a warning list

누가 이런 말을 하면서 여러분들한테 대화를 건다? 음, 레드플래그입니다. 피하십시오. 피곤해집니다.

Nuga ireon mareul hamyeonseo yeoreobundeulhante daehwareul geonda? Eum, redeu peullaegimnida. Pihasipsio. Pigonhaejimnida.

“If someone starts a conversation with you by saying things like this? Hmm, that’s a red flag. Avoid them. It’ll be exhausting.”

  • -(으)면서 means “while doing” or “as [someone] does.” Here, the person uses these phrases while initiating a conversation.
  • 대화를 걸다 is a set expression meaning “to start/initiate a conversation with someone.” It is not literally “hang a conversation.”
  • 피하십시오 is a formal, firm imperative of 피하다 (“avoid”). Its exaggerated form adds comic emphasis.
  • 피곤해집니다 = 피곤하다 (“be tired/exhausting”) + -아/어지다 (“become”). “It becomes tiring.”

Scene 1: the colleague who contradicts themself

소개팅 앱으로 사람 만나는 거 자기는 이해 안 된다고 했던 것 같은데?

Sogaeting aebeuro saram mannaneun geo jagineun ihae an doendago haetdeon geot gateunde?

“I thought they said they couldn’t understand meeting people through dating apps.”

  • 소개팅 앱으로: through / using a dating-app service. (으)로 marks a means or method.
  • 사람 만나는 거: “meeting people”; the verb phrase is nominalized with colloquial 거 (= 것).
  • 이해 안 된다고: reported speech, “(they) said it didn’t make sense / they couldn’t understand it.” The negative 안 comes before the verb.
  • 했던 것 같은데?: “I think [they] said/did that, though?” -던 often recalls a past or previously ongoing/repeated situation; 것 같다 softens the claim into “seems / I think.”

The transcript then uses 자만추, an abbreviation for 자연스러운 만남 추구 (“seeking a natural/organic meeting”). It commonly contrasts with meeting through a matchmaking service or dating app.

Scene 1: hearsay and surprise

알고 보니까 김 대리 소개팅 엄청 한대.

Algo bonikka Gim daeri sogaeting eomcheong handae.

“It turns out Assistant Manager Kim goes on loads of blind dates, apparently.”

  • 알고 보니까: “when I found out / it turns out.” This frames a discovery that changes the speaker’s understanding.
  • 김 대리: “Assistant Manager Kim” or “Kim, the company employee with the title 대리.” Korean job titles are commonly used with names instead of Mr./Ms.
  • 엄청: “extremely / a lot,” an informal intensifier.
  • 한대 here is colloquial reported speech, roughly 한다고 해 (“I hear that [they] do”). The exact subject is inferred from context.

Scene 2: an intern is “overstepping”

아니 진짜 이러면 우리가 뭐가 되냐고. 왜 이렇게 나대?

Ani jinjja ireomyeon uriga mwoga doenyago. Wae ireoke nadae?

“Seriously, what does that make us, then? Why are they acting so over-the-top / overstepping like this?”

  • 이러면 = 이렇다 (“be like this”) + conditional -(으)면 (“if”).
  • 우리가 뭐가 되냐고 is an emotionally charged rhetorical question: “What are we supposed to look like / what does that make us?” The final -냐고 can quote or echo a question.
  • 나대다 is a negative judgement: to act too conspicuously, butt in, show off, or behave as though one’s role/status gives one more license than others think appropriate.
Pragmatic nuance: 나대다 is not an objective description like “speak a lot.” It usually signals the speaker’s disapproval. The same behaviour could be described more positively as 적극적이다 (“proactive”) or 자신감이 있다 (“confident”).

Scene 3: passing contradictory stories between two people

어디 가서 나한테 들었다고는 말하지 말고.

Eodi gaseo nahante deureotdagoneun malhaji malgo.

“Don’t go telling people that you heard it from me.”

  • 어디 가서 literally means “go somewhere and…,” but often means “elsewhere / to other people.”
  • 나한테 = “from me / to me,” depending on the verb. With 듣다 (“hear”), it means the source: “heard from me.”
  • 들었다고 = 듣다 (hear) + past -었- + reported speech -다고.
  • 말하지 말고 = 말하다 (“say”) + negative command -지 말다 (“don’t do”) + -고, which can connect to what follows or leave the instruction hanging.

This phrase is a classic secrecy disclaimer in the podcast’s examples. It can signal trust, but in a gossip context it also helps the speaker avoid accountability.

Scene 3: “It can’t be true!”

그럴 리가 없는데?

Geureol riga eomneunde?

“There’s no way that’s true, though?” / “That can’t be!”

  • 그럴 리가 없다 is a fixed pattern meaning “there is no possibility/reason that [it] would be so.”
  • 그럴 = 그러하다 / 그렇다 used attributively before 리 (“possibility / reason”).
  • -는데? can express contrast, surprise, or an unfinished thought inviting a response.

Speech style: why the transcript sounds conversational

Casual 뭐가 되냐고, 왜 이렇게 나대?, 말도 안 돼

Polite explanatory 알려 드리려고 합니다, 사용하시면 됩니다

Formal / deliberately emphatic 피하십시오

The host shifts between casual examples, polite teaching language, and exaggerated commands for humour. In Korean, register changes are part of the meaning: they help establish whether the speaker is teaching, joking, quoting a character, or judging someone.

Three distinctions worth remembering

1. -다고 vs. -대

그렇게 말했다고 해 is a more explicit reported statement (“they say [someone] said that”). 한대 is a compressed colloquial form of 한다고 해 (“I hear they do”). In fast speech, the endings can be hard to distinguish, so use context.

2. -는데요 is not just “but”

It can set up background, soften an explanation, signal contrast, or leave the conversation open. Translate its discourse function, not just the ending.

3. Korean often omits recoverable subjects

In 엄청 한대, the subject “they” is understood from the previous line. Korean speakers regularly leave out pronouns when the context supplies them.

05 Idioms, slang, and word formation

뒤에서 호박씨 까다

dwieseo hobakssi kkada

Literally, “to crack pumpkin seeds behind [someone’s] back.” In this episode it describes a person who presents a respectable or kind face publicly but behaves differently in private. The key idea is hypocrisy or two-faced behaviour, not merely gossip itself.

Example: 앞에서는 친한 척하더니 뒤에서는 호박씨를 까더라.
Apeseoneun chinhan cheok-hadeoni dwieseo hobakssireul kkadeora.
“They acted friendly to my face, but talked behind my back.”

가식 떨다: why 떨다?

The host explicitly contrasts this 떨다 with the more familiar verb “to tremble/shake.” In expressions such as 가식 떨다, 엄살 떨다, and 호들갑 떨다, it means to put on or display a conspicuous, often excessive performance.

Nuance: the first three are usually critical because they frame the behaviour as excessive or affected. 수다 떨다 is a normal, often friendly expression; do not assume 떨다 always makes a phrase negative.

철벽 치다: “put up an iron wall”

철벽 literally means “iron wall.” 철벽 치다 means to shut down advances so firmly that the other person sees no opening. The episode focuses on dating, but the broader metaphor is creating a strong barrier.

Examples:

  • 그 사람은 관심 없는 사람한테 철벽을 쳐. Geu sarameun gwansim eomneun saramhante cheolbyeogeul chyeo. — “They shut down people they aren’t interested in.”
  • 철벽 치는 것 같아서 더는 연락 안 했어. Cheolbyeok chineun geot gataseo deoneun yeollak an haesseo. — “They seemed to be shutting me out, so I didn’t contact them again.”

철벽남 / 철벽녀 label a man/woman who puts up that wall. These labels can be playful, admiring, or critical depending on context.

나대다: an evaluative verb, not an objective category

The host makes an important sociolinguistic point: the same person can be judged as 적극적이다 (“proactive”), 자신감이 있다 (“confident”), or 나댄다 (“oversteps / shows off”), depending on the observer and social context.

적당히 좀 나대라.Jeokdanghi jom nadaera.“Tone it down / stop overdoing it.” Rude, scolding.

Because 나대다 encodes disapproval, it can reveal more about the speaker’s attitude than about the target’s actual behaviour. Avoid using it casually about someone unless you intend that judgement.

이간질: the suffix-like use of 질

이간 is a Sino-Korean element meaning to separate or create distance between people. 이간질하다 means to sow discord by saying different things to different people or encouraging them to distrust one another.

The host explains -질 as a form used in some nouns for an action regarded as low, bad, or contemptible. Examples in the transcript include:

WordRomanizationMeaning
도둑질dodukjilthe act of stealing / theft
싸움질ssaumjilfighting, picking fights
손가락질songarakjilpointing a finger at someone; by extension, blaming or condemning them
정치질jeongchijilpolitical manoeuvring or “playing politics,” often pejorative

This is a useful pattern, not a rule that every word ending in 질 is automatically negative. Learn each word as a vocabulary item and attend to its established meaning.

손민수하다: a character name becomes a verb

According to the podcast, the expression comes from Son Minsu, a character in Cheese in the Trap, who copies the protagonist’s style and choices. The name became a colloquial verb meaning to copy someone’s look, habits, possessions, or purchases.

  • 손민수하다: to copy someone’s style/choices.
  • 손민수템: an item someone buys because an admired person owns or uses it.
  • Its tone depends on context: it can accuse someone of copying, or playfully describe fan behaviour.

Example: 최애가 쓰는 향수라서 나도 손민수했어.
Choiaega sseuneun hyangsura-seo nado sonminsuhaesseo.
“It’s the perfume my favourite uses, so I got the same one.”

07 Discourse and cultural analysis

1. Gossip is framed as both social risk and intimacy

The host initially calls gossip a “red flag” and says she tries not to participate. Later, she considers how sharing frustrations with a trusted partner may create a feeling of safety and solidarity. These positions are not necessarily contradictory: the episode distinguishes gossip that harms or manipulates someone from private venting that seeks comfort.

2. “Behind their back” language builds a moral narrative

Expressions such as 뒤에서 호박씨 까다, 가식 떨다, and 이간질하다 do more than describe speech. They frame the speaker as hypocritical, deceptive, or deliberately divisive. These words carry judgement; translating them as neutral “talk” would lose the point.

3. 나대다 shows how social norms are policed through language

The host explicitly notes that “overstepping” has no single objective behavioural definition. A confident intern may be viewed as proactive by one person and as 나대는 by another. The word can therefore police perceived status, turn-taking, modesty, and expectations about who is entitled to speak.

4. Reported speech lets a gossiping speaker distance themself

Repeated structures such as 들었다는데, 말하고 다닌다던데, and 어디 가서 나한테 들었다고는 말하지 말고 mark information as something heard from someone else. That can communicate uncertainty, but it can also let the speaker spread a claim while avoiding direct responsibility for it.

5. The “shared dislikes” thesis is the host’s reflection

In the closing section, the host suggests that shared dislikes and moral judgements may reveal compatibility in a way that shared hobbies do not. She uses a fictional villain as an example: if partners disagree about whether a character’s behaviour is wrong, the disagreement may expose a difference in values. This is the host’s personal interpretation of the study, not a universal rule about relationships.

Ethical language note

This episode teaches expressions that can be socially damaging if used carelessly. For active speaking, prioritise neutral descriptions such as 그 행동이 불편했어 (“That behaviour made me uncomfortable”) or 그 말은 좀 무례한 것 같아 (“I think that comment was a bit rude”) when you want to describe a problem without escalating into insults.

08 Key patterns summary

Reported speech

그렇게 말했다고 해. — “They say [someone] said that.”

엄청 한대. — “I hear they do it a lot.”

나한테 들었다고 말하지 마. — “Don’t say you heard it from me.”

Judging behaviour

왜 이렇게 나대? — “Why are they acting so over-the-top?”

가식 떠는 것 같아. — “They seem to be putting on a fake act.”

선을 넘는 것 같아. — “It feels like they’re crossing a line.”

Relationship and emotion

마음이 잘 통해. — “We understand each other well.”

속마음을 털어놓다. — “Open up about one’s true feelings.”

관계가 돈독해지다. — “The relationship/bond grows stronger.”

09 Review quiz

Try answering before opening each explanation.

1. What is the difference between 뒷담화 and 이간질?

Answer: 뒷담화 is negative talk about someone who is absent. 이간질 is actively sowing discord between people, often by telling each side something that damages their relationship.

2. Why is 나대다 more judgemental than 적극적이다?

Answer: 적극적이다 means proactive/active and can be positive. 나대다 frames the behaviour as excessive, attention-seeking, intrusive, or beyond the person’s place.

3. What does 뒤에서 호박씨 까다 mean in context?

Answer: To act one way to someone’s face and behave or speak differently behind their back; to be two-faced or hypocritical.

4. Break down 말하지 말고.

Answer: 말하다 (“say”) + -지 말다 (“don’t do”) + connective -고. In context: “Don’t tell [them]…” / “Don’t say it, and…”

5. What is the function of -다고 in 들었다고?

Answer: It marks reported content: “that [someone] heard [it].” In 나한테 들었다고 말하지 마, it means “Don’t say that you heard it from me.”

6. What does 수다 떨다 mean, and why is it an important exception?

Answer: It means “to chat / talk away.” Unlike 가식 떨다, 엄살 떨다, and 호들갑 떨다, it is not inherently negative.

7. How can 손민수하다 be used positively?

Answer: A fan may say they “손민수했다” an item because their favourite celebrity owns or uses it. It can mean copying an admired person’s purchase rather than accusing someone of malicious imitation.

8. Translate: 내가 오늘 좀 괜히 너무 나댔나?

Answer: “Was I a bit too much / did I overdo it today for no reason?” The speaker is reflecting critically on their own behaviour.

9. What does -ㄹ수록 express in 많이 나눌수록?

Answer: “The more [they] share…” It sets up a proportional relationship: the more one thing happens, the more another tends to happen.

10. Give a less insulting alternative to 나대지 마.

Answer: Depending on the situation: 조금만 차례를 지켜 줄래? (“Could you please wait your turn a little?”) or 그 부분은 내가 설명할게. (“I’ll explain that part.”) These address the behaviour without labelling the person.
